# Pulmonary Mechanics: fact-check report

Scope: the lecture is based on Guyton & Hall 15e, pp. 501–507 (Ch. "Pulmonary Ventilation"). Checked 2026-10-09.

**About access.** NCBI Bookshelf/StatPearls and PMC block automated readers with a CAPTCHA, and the Guyton text is not openly available. Where a value is attributed to Guyton, I confirmed it through lecture handouts that cite Guyton & Hall page by page (mainly J.P. Fisher, Univ. Maryland, citing G&H 11e). Guyton's numbers in this chapter have not changed across editions 11–15. "UNVERIFIED" means no source I could open settled the point.

Verdicts: **TRUE**, **TRUE-N** (true with nuance), **FALSE**, **UNVERIFIED**.

---

### 1. Guyton lung volumes — TRUE
VT 500, IRV 3000, ERV 1100, RV 1200; IC 3500, FRC 2300, VC 4600, TLC 5800 mL. All volumes and capacities are about 20–25% less in women. The arithmetic checks out (IC = IRV+VT, FRC = ERV+RV, VC = IRV+VT+ERV, TLC = VC+RV).
Sources: Fisher, *Pulmonary Ventilation* lecture (cites G&H 11e) https://terpconnect.umd.edu/~jpfisher/index_files/lecture9.pdf ; Mustansiriyah Univ. lecture citing Guyton & Hall 2011, includes the "20–25% less in women" statement: https://www.uomustansiriyah.edu.iq/media/lectures/6/6_2018_03_12!12_54_07_AM.docx

### 2. Guyton pleural and alveolar pressures — TRUE. Figure source — UNVERIFIED (probably Silverthorn)
- Guyton (cm H2O): pleural pressure is −5 at the start of inspiration (FRC) and about −7.5 at end inspiration. Alveolar pressure is about −1 in inspiration and +1 in expiration. About 0.5 L is inspired in about 2 s and expired in 2–3 s. Source: Fisher lecture above (slides 3–4, citing G&H).
- The lecture figure (alveolar ±1, intrapleural −3 → −6, 0–0.5 L, 0–4 s, axis "mm Hg") matches the style and values of **Silverthorn, *Human Physiology: An Integrated Approach*, Ch. 17 "Mechanics of Breathing", figure "Pressure changes during quiet breathing"** (Fig. 17.10/17.11 depending on edition). Its pressures are **mm Hg relative to atmospheric (0 = 760 mm Hg)**. Silverthorn's test-bank items give a typical intrapleural pressure of −3 mm Hg. I could not open the book, so the attribution and figure number are UNVERIFIED.
- **Unit caveat for the slide:** −3/−6 mm Hg ≈ −4/−8 cm H2O, whereas Guyton gives −5/−7.5 cm H2O. Do not show the figure next to Guyton's numbers without labelling units. Other textbooks use −4 mm Hg at rest (OpenStax: https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/22-3-the-process-of-breathing).

### 3. Compliance — TRUE
Lungs alone ≈ 200 mL/cm H2O. Lungs + thorax ≈ 110 mL/cm H2O, about half of the lungs-alone value, because the chest wall must also be stretched.
Sources: Fisher lecture (200, citing G&H); Mustansiriyah notes ("100–110" combined, "200–220" lungs); Deranged Physiology https://derangedphysiology.com/main/cicm-primary-exam/respiratory-system/Chapter-031/static-dynamic-and-specific-compliance (CL ≈ 200, CCW ≈ 200, CRS ≈ 100).

### 4. Surface tension and Laplace — TRUE-N
- 72 (water) / 50 (alveolar fluid without surfactant) / 5–30 dynes/cm (with surfactant) are Guyton's figures. Fisher lecture: "from 50 dynes/cm … to between 5 and 30 dynes/cm"; 72 dyn/cm for water/saline is standard.
- P = 2T/r holds for an alveolus, which has a single air–liquid interface. A soap bubble has two surfaces and obeys P = 4T/r. Fisher lecture: "Pressure = 2 x surface tension / alveolar radius".
- Guyton's worked example (r ≈ 100 µm): about 4 cm H2O (≈3 mm Hg) with normal surfactant, and **about 18 cm H2O (4.5× as great) if the alveolus were lined with *pure water***. The 18 cm H2O value is therefore for pure water (72 dyn/cm), not for "alveolar fluid without surfactant" (50 dyn/cm). The 18 cm/4.5× wording is confirmed only through a flashcard reproduction of Guyton, so the exact phrase is unverified. Note that P = 2T/r with 72 dyn/cm and r = 100 µm gives about 14.7 cm H2O, so Guyton's figure is approximate.

### 5. Surfactant — TRUE-N (three corrections)
- Secreted by type II cells, stored in lamellar bodies, main component DPPC, proteins SP-A, B, C, D: TRUE. Han & Mallampalli 2015, *Ann Am Thorac Soc* https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4418337/
- **"Type II cells ≈10% of alveolar surface"** is the figure quoted from Guyton. Morphometric literature gives **≈5–7%**: type II cells are about 60% of alveolar epithelial cells and about 15% of lung parenchymal cells, but cover only about 5% of the surface. Fehrenbach 2001, *Respir Res* https://respiratory-research.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/rr36 ; Han & Mallampalli (≈7%).
- **Composition:** ≈90% lipid / ≈10% protein by mass (Fehrenbach; Han & Mallampalli). The lecture's **"8% protein, 2% carbohydrate" is not supported**. No standard source lists a separate carbohydrate fraction; SP-A and SP-D are glycoproteins counted within the protein fraction. Use "≈90% lipid, ≈10% protein".
- **Turnover:** about 85% of secreted surfactant is taken back up and recycled by type II cells, and spent material is removed by alveolar macrophages (Fehrenbach) — TRUE. **The "half-life 2 days" figure depends on context.** Adult alveolar pools turn over in **hours**: median 7.6 h, range 1.8–20.8 h, for nebulised surfactant phospholipid in adults (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8886955/), and about 3–4 h for DPPC in adult rats. In **preterm infants** the half-life of endogenous surfactant PC is about **2–4 days** (47 h linoleate-labelled, 95 h palmitate-labelled; https://air.uniud.it/handle/11390/1100668). Say "hours in adults; ~2–4 days in preterm neonates".

### 6. Gestational timing and steroids — TRUE-N
- Small amounts of surfactant are present from **≈22–24 weeks** (60% of gestation). Schittny 2017, *Cell Tissue Res* https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00441-016-2545-0 . Guyton is commonly quoted as "between the 6th and 7th months"; I could not open the text to confirm. "25th week" is acceptable as an approximation.
- **Adequate by ≈34–35 weeks:** the L/S ratio rises after 32–33 weeks, and a ratio ≥2 (≥2.4 in some labs) indicates maturity. RDS incidence is about 5% at 34 weeks and <1% at 37 weeks (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infant_respiratory_distress_syndrome).
- **Glucocorticoids accelerate surfactant production and lung maturation.** Calling them "essential" overstates it. ACOG recommends one course at 24+0 to 33+6 weeks (consider from 22–23 weeks, and at 34+0 to 36+6 weeks for late-preterm risk). Summary: https://cdn.bcm.edu/sites/default/files/antenatal-corticosteroids.pdf
- IRDS (hyaline membrane disease) in premature infants: TRUE.

### 7. Surfactant functions — TRUE
Surfactant lowers surface tension more in small alveoli, because the film is compressed and its concentration rises. This stabilises alveoli of unequal size, prevents atelectasis, increases compliance, lowers the work of breathing, and helps keep alveoli dry by reducing the suction that draws fluid into them. Sources: StatPearls "Physiology, Pulmonary Compliance" https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538324/ (read only via search excerpt because of the CAPTCHA); Mustansiriyah notes.

### 8. Elastic recoil: 1/3 tissue, 2/3 surface tension — TRUE
Fisher lecture (citing G&H): "surface tension of the alveolar fluid contributes 2/3 of the total lung elasticity". Historically, von Neergaard (1929) put the surface share at 2/3–3/4.

### 9. Work of breathing — TRUE
Guyton: 3–5% of total body energy in quiet breathing, rising up to about 50-fold in heavy exercise. The three components (compliance/elastic work, tissue-resistance work, airway-resistance work) are correct, and the work rises with lower compliance or higher resistance. The lecture's "2–5%" is acceptable. Source: Guyton-derived handout quoting "only 3 to 5 per cent of the total energy" (search excerpt); Fisher lecture.

### 10. Airway resistance — TRUE (one nuance on heliox)
- The greatest resistance is in the larger bronchioles and bronchi near the trachea (Guyton), or medium-sized bronchi up to about the 7th generation (West). Small bronchioles contribute little because their total cross-section is huge. Fisher lecture: "does not occur in the alveoli, but in the larger bronchioles and bronchi"; VT open text https://pressbooks.lib.vt.edu/pulmonaryphysiology/chapter/airflow-and-airway-resistance/
- R = 8ηL/πr⁴ applies to laminar flow. Laminar resistance depends on viscosity and turbulent resistance on density. Reynolds number Re = 2rvd/η, with turbulence above about 2000.
- Heliox is **much less dense but slightly MORE viscous** than air, so it helps where flow is turbulent and gives no advantage where flow is laminar. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliox . The VT open text wrongly says heliox has "lower viscosity"; do not copy that.

### 11. Bronchomotor control — TRUE-N (one claim unverified/likely wrong)
- Vagal ACh via M3 → constriction; histamine and SRS-A (leukotrienes C4/D4/E4) from mast cells → constriction; irritants → reflex and local constriction; β2 (mainly circulating epinephrine, since sympathetic innervation of human airway smooth muscle is sparse) → dilation: TRUE (VT open text; Guyton).
- Serotonin → constriction: true in asthmatics and animals, with a minor role in humans. α-adrenergic constriction: minor. NO → dilation: TRUE (it mediates the NANC bronchodilator response), but inhaled NO is only a weak bronchodilator.
- **CO2:** low airway/alveolar PCO2 → **bronchoconstriction**, high PCO2 → bronchodilation, a direct effect on smooth muscle that helps match ventilation to perfusion. The lecture's "↑CO2 → dilation" is correct. Sources: VT text ("Low airway PCO2 … has a direct … bronchoconstrictive effect"); hypocapnia review https://eclass.uniwa.gr/modules/document/file.php/FTHER105/8.%20HYPOCAPNIA%20AND%20ASTHMA%20BREATHING%20RETRAINING.pdf
- **"Decreased O2 in small airways → dilation": UNVERIFIED and not standard textbook physiology.** The classic local response to alveolar hypoxia is pulmonary **vaso**constriction. Direct effects of hypoxia on airways are weak and inconsistent: isolated human bronchi relax, but in vivo hypoxia potentiated methacholine bronchoconstriction in asthmatics. Recommend deleting the claim or replacing it with "↓PCO2 → bronchoconstriction".

### 12. Lung volume and airway resistance; emphysema — TRUE
Radial traction (tethering) widens airways as lung volume rises, so resistance falls. Loss of traction in emphysema promotes dynamic collapse and air trapping. VT open text, chapters 5–6 (https://pressbooks.lib.vt.edu/pulmonaryphysiology/chapter/dynamic-airway-compression/).

### 13. Chest wall resting volume — TRUE-N
The relaxed (resting) volume of the chest wall is **≈60% of TLC**, or about 55–60% of VC (ERS SpirXpert "Elastic properties of lung and thorax": "nearly 60% of … TLC"; UT Dallas speech-mechanics notes: "~55% of VC"). The lecture's 75% is the upper end of quoted values. Above that volume both lung and chest wall recoil inward. At FRC (about 35–40% TLC) their recoils are equal and opposite, and intrapleural pressure is about −5 cm H2O (Guyton). https://spirxpert.ers-education.org/en/lung-growth-aging/elastic-properties-of-lung-and-thorax

### 14. Quiet inspiration active, expiration passive — TRUE-N
Correct. Guyton notes quiet breathing is accomplished "almost entirely" by the diaphragm, with the diaphragm giving about 75% of the tidal volume change, so the external intercostals contribute little at rest. VT open text: "during quiet resting breathing, expiration is passive".

### 15. Hysteresis — TRUE-N
Static hysteresis comes mainly from surface tension and surfactant dynamics; a saline-filled lung shows much less. Alveolar recruitment–derecruitment and tissue stress relaxation also contribute. The area of the dynamic loop represents work against non-elastic (airway plus tissue) resistance. Deranged Physiology (link in #3) lists the causes as "surfactant, tissue relaxation, recruitment/derecruitment". The Mustansiriyah notes attribute hysteresis to "active vs passive", which is not the mechanism.

### 16. Flow–volume loop — TRUE-N
PEF occurs early (in the first ~20–30% of FVC) and depends on effort. Beyond that, maximal flow is effort-independent because of dynamic airway compression downstream of the equal-pressure point. "Last ~2/3 of FVC" is a reasonable teaching approximation, not a fixed boundary. FEF25–75 is "relatively" effort-independent but highly variable. LITFL https://partone.litfl.com/spirometry.html ; Loyola https://www.lumen.luc.edu/lumen/meded/medicine/pulmonar/physio/pf2.htm . Guyton's "maximum expiratory flow" explanation (pressure on the outside of the bronchioles compresses them, so extra effort cannot raise flow) is consistent.

### 17. FEV1/FVC — TRUE-N. Costanzo numbers — UNVERIFIED
- Normal is about 80% (Guyton; BRS/Costanzo "FEV1/FVC = 0.8"). GOLD defines COPD by post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC <0.70 and still prefers the fixed ratio over the LLN (GOLD 2025 summary https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12097743/). **ERS/ATS 2022 instead define obstruction as FEV1/FVC < LLN (z < −1.645) and advise against the 0.70 fixed cut-off** (https://publications.ersnet.org/content/erj/60/1/2101499).
- Restrictive: ↓FVC with normal or ↑ ratio only *suggests* restriction. **True restriction requires TLC < LLN**, measured as a lung volume (ERS/ATS 2022).
- Costanzo example (4.0/5.0 = 0.8; asthma 1.3/3.1 = 0.42; fibrosis 2.8/3.1 = 0.90): the numbers are internally consistent. Costanzo's *BRS Physiology* Fig. 4.2 shows exactly these three panels (normal / obstructive (asthma) / restrictive (fibrosis)), and the 80% / 42% / 90% ratios appear in the Guyton-based Mustansiriyah notes. I could not see the exact litre values in any source I could open: UNVERIFIED.

### 18. Fibrosis vs emphysema — TRUE-N (one error)
Fibrosis: ↓compliance, ↓TLC, ↓FRC, **↓RV**. MSD Manual lists restrictive disease as RV "Decreased" (https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/characteristic-physiologic-changes-associated-with-pulmonary-disorders). **The lecture's "RV unaffected" is wrong.** RV usually falls, though less than TLC, so RV/TLC may be normal or raised. Emphysema: ↑compliance, ↑TLC, ↑FRC, ↑RV (air trapping) — TRUE.

### 19. Clinical cases — TRUE (minor nuances)
(a) Unilateral phrenic transection: ipsilateral hemidiaphragm paralysis, paradoxical upward motion on sniff fluoroscopy (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8958460/), and a modest fall in VC that is worse supine. Many patients are asymptomatic.
(b) Open pneumothorax: Ppl = Patm, the lung collapses from its own recoil, and the chest wall springs outward (BRS/Costanzo; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intrapleural_pressure).
(c) Pleural effusion: compression and a restrictive (extraparenchymal) pattern.
(d) Loss of surfactant: ↓compliance, atelectasis, ↑work of breathing (IRDS; drowning by washout and inactivation).
(e) Emphysema (↑compliance) vs fibrosis (↓compliance).
(f) Pulmonary oedema in LV failure: ↓compliance and ↑elastic work. Nuance: bronchial-wall oedema can also raise airway resistance ("cardiac asthma"), so the physiology may be mixed rather than purely restrictive (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiac_asthma).

### 20. Water-seal spirometer — TRUE
On expiration the bell rises. With the pen on the counterweight side, inspiration (bell falls) records as an **upward** deflection and expiration as downward. San Diego Miramar College physiology text, Ch. 18: "The upward movement of the recording pen occurs during any inspiration" (https://sdmiramar.edu/sites/default/files/2024-04/Physiol%20Text%20Ch%2018%20Respiratory%20System.pdf). Note: a pen attached directly to the bell gives the opposite polarity. RV (and therefore FRC and TLC) cannot be measured by simple spirometry; it is measured by helium dilution (Guyton: FRC = (Ci/Cf − 1)·Vspir; Fisher lecture), N2 washout, or body plethysmography.

### 21. Helium–oxygen — TRUE-N
Medical use: lower density reduces resistance where flow is turbulent (upper-airway obstruction, croup). It buys time and has no proven effect on outcomes. Diving use: the **main reason is avoiding nitrogen narcosis** (and keeping O2 fraction low at depth); lower density reducing the work of breathing at depth is secondary. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliox
